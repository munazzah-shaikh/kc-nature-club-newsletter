June Roundup

World Environment Day 2026 | 5th June

“Inspired by Nature. For Climate. For Our Future.” This year’s theme calls on communities everywhere to look to nature itself for climate answers — restoring forests, wetlands and mangroves rather than relying solely on technological fixes. Nature-based solutions have moved from a side conversation to the centre of the global climate response.

Mangroves, forests and wetlands do double duty as carbon sinks and as natural buffers — mangrove roots dissipate wave energy along coastlines, forests regulate rainfall and stabilise soil, and wetlands filter water while storing carbon in their sediment for centuries. India has folded this thinking into initiatives like the Green India Mission and its expanding network of coastal afforestation projects.

Did You Know?A 500-metre strip of healthy mangrove forest can reduce incoming wave height by 50–99%, acting as a natural shield against floods and cyclones. Yet the world has lost nearly half its mangrove cover in the last 40 years. Mumbai still holds large mangrove patches at Gorai, Versova, Thane Creek and Navi Mumbai — making their protection a local priority as much as a global one.

26 July 2026

Mangrove Action Day Webinar

26th July marks the International Day for the Conservation of the Mangrove Ecosystem (UNESCO, 2015). The club hosted a live online Q&A with Mr. Stalin D., Director of Vanashakti — one of Mumbai’s foremost mangrove and wetland conservation organisations.

Into the Mangroves: Thane Creek Flamingo Sanctuary Trail

Students walked the Nature Trail near Bhandup Pumping Station and observed a rich mix of mangrove flora. They learned how mangrove roots adapt to muddy soil and how salt glands keep the plant’s salt balance in check.

Species Spotlight

The Indian Elephant

Elephas maximus indicus — Endangered on the IUCN Red List

An estimated wild population of around 27,000–29,000 individuals remains in India — the largest population of Asian elephants anywhere in the world.

As a keystone species, elephants shape the forests they live in: dispersing seeds across vast distances, creating clearings that let sunlight reach the forest floor, and digging waterholes that other species depend on during dry seasons.

Elephants rely on wildlife corridors — traditional migratory routes connecting fragmented forest patches. As these corridors shrink under pressure from agriculture, roads, railways and settlements, human–elephant conflict has intensified in states like Odisha, West Bengal, Assam and Karnataka.

World Elephant Day (12 August) exists to spotlight both the urgency of elephant conservation and the shared responsibility of coexistence.

How to Protect It

  • Support organisations working to conserve and restore wildlife corridors
  • Avoid products linked to elephant exploitation, including illegal ivory and unethical tourism rides
  • Report wildlife crime or poaching activity to local forest department helplines
  • Choose responsible, ethical ecotourism operators that prioritise animal welfare
  • Spread awareness about human–elephant coexistence in your own community

Myth vs Fact — Mosquito Awareness Day (20 August)

World Mosquito Day marks the day in 1897 when Sir Ronald Ross discovered that female Anopheles mosquitoes transmit malaria. The day raises awareness of mosquito-borne diseases and how to protect ourselves.

❌ MYTH
✅ FACT
All mosquitoes spread diseases.
Only certain mosquito species can transmit specific diseases.
Mosquitoes live only in dirty places.
Many mosquitoes breed in clean, stagnant water too.
Only female mosquitoes bite.
Female mosquitoes bite because they need blood nutrients to develop eggs.
Male mosquitoes bite humans.
Male mosquitoes mainly feed on nectar and plant juices.
Mosquitoes can fly very far.
Most mosquitoes usually stay fairly close to their breeding areas.
Mosquitoes are active only at night.
Some species are active during the day, others more at night.
Mosquitoes are attracted only to light.
They are attracted by CO₂, body heat, and odours.
Mosquitoes are completely useless.
They are part of food webs and can contribute to pollination.
